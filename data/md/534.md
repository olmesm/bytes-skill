### The masculine urge to multiplex everything

I usually try and avoid the type of developers who talk about how much they love tmux, you know because of the neck beard and odor situations. But I got access to [Rex public beta](https://www.superlogical.com/updates/public-testing-beginning) yesterday because I ~~have special influencer access~~ was one of the lucky 12,000 people who got access, and as a Ghostty enjoyer, I couldn’t say no.

**So what is Rex?**

Rex, much like tmux, is a multiplexer that comes with a client / server system and its own terminal app [powered by libghostty](https://ghostty.org/docs/about#libghostty). We all *definitely* know what a multiplexer is, but just so we’re on the same page, a multiplexer combines multiple data sources into a single channel, allowing a receiving system to read and process each stream through one shared connection. Rex currently connects all of your terminals via sessions, but in the future you’ll be able to connect all of your dev tools and even things like Slack or a browser.

In practice, this gives you a control center that lets tools and agents read output from one terminal and send input to another through a shared interface.

*Here’s what Rex can do today:*

- **Programmable workspaces:** Create, organize, and control named terminal sessions through commands and APIs, allowing scripts and AI agents to launch programs, read output, and send input to specific terminals. It also lets you do things like have `codex` send prompts to and approve commands in `claude` and vice versa.
- **Session continuity:** Keep sessions and programs running on their host independently of your connection, so you can disconnect and return to the same work from another device.
- **Runtime observability:** Inspect terminal output and program activity, including progress and blocked states reported by supported programs, to understand what’s happening and what needs attention.

**Bottom line:** Sexy Rexy is going to make multiplexing mainstream in 2027, you heard it here first 🫡.

## Our Friends (With Benefits)

![Woman smiles and says, Why would I deceive you? There's no reason for me to do that.](https://bytes.dev/images/content/why-would-i-deceive-you.jpeg)

Your database when it inserts a timestamp

### [Your database lies to you about time](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pHrxmSjxLg8)

Not really what you want from the thing that’s supposed to be THE source of truth in your application. But database timestamps can be misleading. How?

- **Time can stand still:** A transaction’s timestamp may stay fixed even as wall-clock time moves forward.
- **Creation order isn’t commit order:** A transaction that starts first can finish last, so a creation timestamp may not reflect commit order.
- **Your worker can miss records:** Advancing a cursor by creation time can skip writes that commit later.

Whether you’re using Convex or not, [this whiteboard session](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pHrxmSjxLg8) will help you better understand what database timestamps actually mean—and how to keep your queues and workers from silently skipping records.

That said, once you use Convex, it’s hard to go back. [Give it a try today](https://www.convex.dev/).
